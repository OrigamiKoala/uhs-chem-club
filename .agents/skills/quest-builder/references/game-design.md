# Designing a side quest that is fun and is chemistry

Contents: 1. Choosing the concept · 2. The fun test · 3. The curve · 4. Starting points by concept ·
5. How chemistry games go wrong

## 1. Choosing the concept

A good side-quest concept passes all five:

1. **It has a physical rule a hand can act out.** Attraction and repulsion, things taking up room,
   counts that must balance, flow from more to less, shapes that fit or do not, order that matters.
2. **It can be seen.** The cause is drawn and moves: charges, particles, bonds, energy as motion. A
   concept that only exists as a number on a readout (molar mass, a rate constant) makes a worksheet.
3. **It needs no earlier chemistry.** A 12-year-old can start with what they know about the everyday
   world. If stage 1 needs the word "mole" or "electron", choose again or start one level lower.
4. **It is deep enough for three movements.** One base rule, then a complication that makes the base
   rule alone give the wrong answer, then sequences or combinations. If you cannot name the three, the
   concept is a single stage.
5. **It is true, and worth knowing.** A chemistry teacher would recognise it as a real idea from a real
   course (Erebus is first-semester organic chemistry). The debrief can name it and nothing in the game
   needs un-teaching later.

Do not pick a concept another contract already covers (Erebus: charge attraction, steric crowding,
mechanism order). A Learn world covering the same topic is fine: the Learn road teaches it rigorously
with real terms; a side quest lets someone feel it with none.

## 2. The fun test

Hold the mechanic, and then every stage, to these. They are why someone keeps playing.

- **Five seconds to the verb.** A new player sees the screen and knows what to try (drag this to that,
  turn this, place these) without reading a paragraph. Stage 1 is solvable by anyone who tries the
  obvious thing, and its payoff shows why that worked.
- **The puzzle is the chemistry.** Playing well means applying the rule. Swap test: replace the
  chemistry with abstract coloured blocks. If the puzzle is unchanged, the chemistry is a skin. In
  Erebus the swap fails, which is right: crowding only blocks a route because atoms have size in 3D.
- **Thinking, not searching.** The answer comes from looking and reasoning, never from trying every
  option. A stage with two possible answers is a coin flip; give the player at least four plausible
  moves, or a sequence, so a guess is unlikely and a miss is informative.
- **A miss teaches and costs nothing.** Instant feedback, a reason that names what happened, retry
  immediately. No lives, no timer that punishes, no lost progress.
- **Every stage has a point.** Each one adds a twist, combines two earlier rules, or is a trap where the
  rule the player just learned gives the wrong answer (Erebus stage 5: both targets pull equally, one is
  walled in). Two stages that differ only in numbers or layout: cut one.
- **The payoff is worth watching.** The solve sets off something physical that is caused by the move:
  pieces snap, a ring springs open, a leaving piece drifts away. Two to four seconds, then control
  returns. The player should want to see the next one.
- **Short stages, visible progress.** One to two minutes each. Something in the world changes for good
  after every clear, and the player can see how far they have come.
- **Room to be good at it.** A first-try solve with no solution hint counts toward the CLEAN streak
  (display only, pays nothing). The last movement should make a clean solve something to be proud of.
- **No reading to play.** Prompt plus scene is enough. Story is two sentences. The long text (the
  debrief) comes after the work, when the player wants to know what they were doing.

Things that feel like game design and are not fun: a quiz with art around it, a memory test of facts, a
dial hunted until a light turns green, a countdown added to make an easy task tense, collecting for its
own sake, a daily obligation.

## 3. The curve

Plan the stages as three or four movements. Erebus:

| Movement | Stages | New rule | Shape of the play |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 to 4 | Red gives, blue receives; the strongest pair wins | One move; then decoys |
| 2 | 5 to 7 | A crowded target is blocked: look from another side | The base rule alone now fails |
| 3 | 8 to 10 | Bystanders: not everything in the scene takes part | More in the scene, same single move |
| 4 | 11 to 20 | Steps happen in order; making room; helpers | Two moves, ordered; rules combine |

Rules for the curve:

- Stage 1 cannot be failed by someone who tries the obvious thing. Stage 2 repeats the verb with one
  visible consequence added.
- Introduce one rule per movement. Show it first in a stage where it is the only thing going on, then
  combine it with earlier rules.
- Put a trap early in each movement, so the new rule is felt as a surprise, not read as a notice.
- After a hard stage, an easier one with a satisfying payoff.
- The last stage uses every rule and feels like a finale; it does not introduce anything.
- An intro card (controls and visual code only) auto-opens on the first stage of each movement and
  nowhere else.

## 4. Starting points by concept

These are prompts for thought, not specifications, and not a list to pick the first item from. Design the
mechanic for the concept; do not reproduce any existing game's levels, look or name.

| Concept (real name, for the debrief) | Physical rule the hand acts out | A mechanic that is that rule |
|---|---|---|
| Conservation of atoms (balancing equations) | Nothing appears or vanishes; pieces only regroup | Break supplied pieces apart and rebuild the wanted products with none left over; choose how many of each to bring |
| Intermolecular attraction, "like dissolves like" | Charged ends hold charged ends; uncharged pieces are left out | Route or pour mixtures through channels lined with charged or uncharged walls to separate them |
| Crystal packing and ionic lattices | Opposite charges sit next to each other, like charges apart, sizes must fit | Fill a frame with two kinds of charged pieces so no like pair touches |
| Molecular shape (electron-pair repulsion) | Things that repel spread as far apart as they can | Attach arms to a centre in 3D and let them settle; predict or build the shape that fits a socket |
| Oxidation and reduction | Charge handed from one piece to another, never lost | Pass charge tokens along a chain of holders of different pull to light a target |
| Acids and bases | A small piece (a proton) passed from a weaker holder to a stronger one | Hand the piece along until a tank is neutral; the wrong order overshoots |
| Equilibrium | Two opposite flows that settle where they match | Open and close gates between two chambers so the flows balance at a target mix |
| Reaction rate and collisions | Only hits that are hard enough and aimed right react | Aim and set speed so pieces meet at the right face; crowding and heat change the odds |
| Gas behaviour | Moving particles push on walls; less room or more speed means more push | Size and heat chambers so a piston or valve reaches a mark without bursting |
| Polymers | The same link, repeated; two-ended pieces chain, three-ended pieces net | Choose and join pieces to grow a chain with a wanted stiffness or stretch |
| Chirality | A shape and its mirror image cannot be laid on each other | Turn 3D pieces to find which of several fit a handed socket |
| Electron shells and periodic trends | Each layer holds only so many; outer layers are held less tightly | A logic puzzle: deduce an unknown piece's layer filling from how it behaves next to known ones |

For each candidate, write the three movements before committing. The best choice is usually the one
whose second movement (where the base rule alone fails) you can state most sharply.

Use 3D where the concept is spatial (shape, crowding, lattices, handedness) and draw flat things flat
(a graph, a level diagram). What is drawn is what is graded.

## 5. How chemistry games go wrong

Check the design against each. The first six have all been built in this repository and fixed at cost
(`PRODUCT.md` section 5 has the full ledger).

| Failure | What it looks like | Do this |
|---|---|---|
| The answer is on the screen | A label, colour or readout states what to pick | The thing under study is found out by acting, not read off |
| Passable without the idea | Brightest-to-brightest works on every stage | Each movement needs a stage where the earlier habit fails |
| The lesson arrives after the fact | The play is abstract and the card carries the chemistry | The cause is visible while acting; the card only names it |
| Nicknames that never resolve | "Givers" and "hungry targets" to the end, the real term never given | Every nickname is tied to its real name on a chemistry card and in the debrief |
| Stiff, textbook copy | Prompts that read like a lab manual; no character, no humour | Erebus's voice: everyday nicknames (hungry target, helper, bystander), short lively sentences |
| A figure of speech that teaches a wrong rule | The nickname implies behaviour the chemistry does not have | The playful word may colour the piece; the move it leads to must be the real one |
| Copy in three places | Stage text edited in one file and stale in two | One source, generated or imported everywhere else |
| Worksheet with a theme | Type the number, press submit | No stage is arithmetic; quantities are compared by looking |
| Colour as the only cue | Red and blue with no second signal | Double every cue with shape, size or motion |
| Difficulty by obscurity | The hard stage is hard to see or to click | Hard means more to reason about, never smaller targets or hidden controls |
| A wall of story | Three paragraphs before stage 1 | Two sentences from Vess, then play |
| The stuck player | No way forward after several misses | Rung 3 shows the move; nothing ever locks |
| False simplification | A rule stated as always true that later chemistry breaks | State the limit in a clause, or choose cases where the rule holds |
